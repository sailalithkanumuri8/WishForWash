The Issue

1 in 4 people still can't count on safe water.

It's easy to think the water crisis is someone else's problem. The numbers say otherwise.

  • 2.1 billion

    people lack safely managed drinking water

  • 3.4 billion

    lack safely managed sanitation

  • 1.7 billion

    lack basic hygiene services at home

Poorer places carry the most: people in the least developed countries are more than twice as likely to lack basic drinking water and sanitation.

Source: WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme, 2025 update

Data note

Our earlier sources cited 26% and 46%, estimates for 2020. We now use the 2025 JMP update, so every number here is current.

What is WASH?

WASH means water, sanitation and hygiene. Each one only works when the others do.

  • WA

    Water

    Safe drinking water at home, available when needed and free from contamination.

  • S

    Sanitation

    A toilet that keeps waste away from people and treats it safely.

  • H

    Hygiene

    Soap, water and a place to wash your hands at home.

Why it happens

Our research points to four forces behind the crisis.

  • Population growth

    More people share the same rivers and aquifers, so water gets scarcer and more polluted.

  • Aging infrastructure

    Pipes wear out. When an old main breaks, safe water stops, as Atlanta saw in 2024.

  • Limited funding

    Water systems are expensive to build and run; researchers call money "a main limitation."

  • Weak water management

    Without good policy, data and governance, water gets overused and polluted.

My name is Whitney Ashley, and I work for Lord Aeck Sargent, the architect of [record] for the Kendeda Building. Today, I'm gonna be talking about the Water Petal and the impact of a Living Building Challenge.

The goal of the Water Petal for the Living Building Challenge is to capture rainfall, and it all starts from here, in the PV array, 913 solar panels. The water hits the PV array, falls down onto the gutter and down the downspout into the rainwater system. And after the water goes through the rooftop drains, it goes through the first flush filter system, and that's where all the big debris, sediment and things like that are removed, and then directed down to the cistern through these pipes.

And this is the final destination where the rainwater ends to become potable drinking water. This is the water treatment center that has the micron stations here and then also the UV treatment center here, which ends up into the day tanks. The potable [water] is included for the sinks, fountains, showers and composting toilets.

Greywater, which doesn't contain organic matter, is from the water fountains, sinks and showers, which is directed into the primary tank for infiltration on-site. This water will then be pumped to the constructed wetland in front of the building, where the water is then gravity fed into the surrounding site and infiltrate back into the soil to recharge the surrounding groundwater.

Our building actually doesn't have a blackwater system, it has a composting toilet system. So it takes the mass from the toilets and brings it down and separates the mass from the liquids. The liquid from the composter goes to the bottom and is actually pumped up to the [leachate] tank. A third-party vendor empties the [leachate] tank about every three months, and then the composting bins usually don't have to be cleaned out for every one or two years based on usage.

Solutions

Every one of these problems has a fix someone is already building.

Flip each card to see the fix and the proof behind it.

  • Not enough clean water to go around

    The fix: wastewater recycling

    Treated, safely reused wastewater becomes a "new" source of clean water.

    Proof: a peer-reviewed review in npj Clean Water.

    Source: npj Clean Water

  • Stormwater rushing polluted runoff into creeks

    The fix: green infrastructure

    Bioswales, rain gardens and permeable surfaces soak up stormwater.

    Proof: EPA expects planned green infrastructure to cut runoff in the Proctor Creek watershed.

    Source: US EPA

  • Nobody knows what's in the water

    The fix: community monitoring

    Chattahoochee Riverkeeper trains volunteers to test urban streams for bacteria.

    Proof: EPA put most of its Proctor Creek study sites at long-running volunteer stations to compare the data.

    Source: US EPA

  • Drought-year irrigation drains rivers

    The fix: smarter irrigation

    Southwest Georgia is shifting drought-year irrigation to deep aquifer wells.

    Proof: a $49.8 million grant funds about 242 deep wells, plus aquifer and stream monitoring.

    Source: Albany State University

  • Sanitation that doesn't fit the community

    The fix: affordable sanitation design

    GT students design low-cost sanitation around what a community needs.

    Proof: EWB-GT's Malawi "Sanitation Solutions" project placed third at the 2024 GAWP student design competition.

    Source: GT CEE

WISH FOR WASH

What we do

  • Research

    Using human-centered research to better understand water, sanitation, and hygiene challenges, and the people most affected by them.

  • Design & Innovation

    Creating practical, affordable sanitation products and systems that are built around real community needs.

  • Education & Advocacy

    Making WASH more approachable through workshops, storytelling, and programs that bring more diverse voices into the field.
